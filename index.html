Transformation Lab

Practise exponential and logarithmic transformations, then transform a real-style medical dataset and back-transform the results. The calculator stays beside you the whole time so you can see every number being made.

Why transform data?

A Student's t-test assumes each group is normally distributed and that the groups have equal variance (homogeneity of variance, HOV). When a dataset is badly skewed, one option is to transform it: apply the same mathematical function to every value so the data are rescaled into a more symmetric shape.

  • Linear transformations (add, subtract, multiply or divide by a constant) move or stretch the data but keep its shape. They never fix skew.
  • Non-linear transformations (log, square root, exponential) change the spacing between values, which is what changes the shape.
  • After analysing transformed data, you back-transform results (means, confidence limits) into the original units so they make sense.

y = eˣe ≈ 2.71828

An exponential transformation raises a base to the power of each data value. In biology the base is almost always Euler's number, e ≈ 2.718. So for the value 2, the transformed value is e² = 2.718 × 2.718 = 7.389.

Exponentials stretch out large values: each step of 0.5 in x makes y jump by more than the step before. That is why eˣ can pull a left-skewed dataset toward symmetry, and why it is the tool for undoing a natural log.

y = log₁₀(x)opposite of 10ˣ

A logarithm answers one question: "What power do I raise the base to, to get this number?" For base 10, log₁₀(1000) = 3 because 10³ = 1000.

Logs compress large values: 1,000 becomes 3, and 10,000 only becomes 4. That is why logs pull in the long tail of right-skewed data.

  • log of 1 is 0 for any base, because anything raised to the power 0 is 1.
  • log of 0 or a negative number is undefined. Try it on the calculator and see what happens.

y = ln(x)log base e · opposite of eˣ

The natural log, written ln (or logₑ), asks: "What power do I raise e to, to get this number?" ln(7.389) = 2 because e² = 7.389.

It is the most common log transformation in biology, and it pairs with eˣ (Excel EXP) for back-transforming. ln gives bigger numbers than log₁₀ for the same x, but the shape is the same: ln(x) is always 2.303 × log₁₀(x).

C-reactive protein at a health screening

Most people have low CRP, but a few have high values. Your job: decide what a typical CRP is for this group.

Why is the geometric mean smaller than the arithmetic mean?

1 · Top line

Each patient's ln(CRP). On this scale the dots are evenly spread. The highest patient (18.5 mg/L) is only 2.92, not far from everyone else.

2 · Grey lines

eˣ carries each value back down to mg/L. The lines fan out to the right: eˣ stretches the high values far apart.

3 · Thick line

The average on the ln scale (0.857) comes down to 2.36 mg/L. The arithmetic mean (4.22) is higher because in mg/L the two high patients pull hard on it.

Geometric mean (via logs)

As the highest value grows, the arithmetic mean races upward but the geometric mean barely moves. (This example uses log₁₀ for easy numbers; ln works exactly the same way.)

Key idea: back-transforming the mean of the logs does not give back the ordinary mean. It gives the geometric mean, a typical value that one or two very high patients can't drag upward.

Why is the interval lopsided in mg/L?

On the ln scale (top) the interval is the same size on both sides of the mean: ± 0.71. eˣ stretches bigger values more than smaller ones, so after back-transforming the upper side stretches to 2.45 mg/L above the geometric mean while the lower side is only 1.20 mg/L below it. Back-transformed intervals are always lopsided like this, and they can never go below 0.

Common mistake: back-transforming the standard error

eSE = e0.3235 = 1.38, but that number has no meaning in mg/L. Building a CI as "mean ± t* × 1.38" gives the wrong interval. Always finish the CI on the ln scale first, then back-transform only the two limits.
